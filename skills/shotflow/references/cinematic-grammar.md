# Five-axis cinematic grammar

Use all five axes for every planned or compiled shot. Write concrete, physically executable decisions.

## 1. Narrative moment

Choose the exact instant: anticipation, action, consequence, discovery, or aftermath. State what changes by the end of the shot.

Good: `The repair cable catches the worker at the instant the sky fissure widens.`

Weak: `Epic dramatic moment.`

## 2. Camera movement

State witness position, shot scale, camera path, subject relationship, and focus behavior. Keep movement physically possible and motivated by the beat.

Good: `A measured lateral track follows the worker left-to-right; the tower remains a fixed depth reference.`

Weak: `Cinematic camera, dynamic lens.`

## 3. Light and color

Name motivated sources, exposure hierarchy, contrast, and color response. Preserve the source direction across a cut unless the story changes it.

Good: `Cold storm skylight remains camera-left; the repair seam adds a narrow amber bounce on wet metal.`

Weak: `Beautiful moody lighting.`

## 4. Space and composition

Define screen direction, geography, foreground/midground/background, subject scale, and the frame's visual center.

Good: `The worker stays right of the fissure and moves screen-right; the tether leads back to the lower-left anchor.`

Weak: `Balanced cinematic composition.`

## 5. Material and physics

Describe weight, surface response, wind, water, inertia, contact, deformation, and causal motion. Reject decorative effects with no source.

Good: `The soaked cape drags downwind; the metal cable goes taut before the body changes direction.`

Weak: `Ultra-realistic textures and particles.`

## Clean-room rule

Build grammar from scene goals and general filmmaking principles. Do not copy named-director fingerprints, film-specific shot descriptions, or prompt libraries. If a user names a reference, translate the desired qualities into these five axes and omit imitation claims.
